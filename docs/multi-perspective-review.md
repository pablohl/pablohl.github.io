# Multi-Perspective Review Guideline

**Purpose:** Evaluate explanatory tools, engineering decision aids, and interactive experiments through several distinct perspectives before deciding what to improve.

The objective is not to maximize features, interactivity, or actionability. It is to improve the clarity, depth, and experience of the tool's central idea.

## Step 1 — Establish the intended discovery

Before reviewing, identify:

- What problem should the reader recognize?
- What distinction or mechanism are we proposing?
- What should the examples or interactions reveal?
- What should the reader understand differently afterward?

Use this as the reference point for all subsequent recommendations.

## Step 2 — Select complementary reviewers

Choose three to five perspectives based on the actual subject and audience.

Include:

- A practitioner who would encounter the problem in real work.
- A less experienced reader who can expose assumptions and accessibility problems.
- A subject-matter expert who can challenge the validity of the reasoning.
- An interaction or information designer who can evaluate how the idea is experienced.

Add or replace roles when appropriate. Avoid perspectives that merely repeat the same questions.

## Step 3 — Review independently

For each perspective, document:

1. **Success criterion:** What would make this useful or convincing?
2. **Questions tested:** Three to five specific questions.
3. **Strengths:** What works, supported by concrete evidence.
4. **Weaknesses:** What doesn't work and why it matters.
5. **Recommendation:** The smallest meaningful improvement.
6. **Limitations:** What was inspected, inferred, or not tested.

Keep reviews independent before performing synthesis.

## Step 4 — Compare and classify findings

Organize findings into:

- **Convergence:** Multiple perspectives identify the same problem.
- **Complementarity:** Different observations reveal a shared underlying issue.
- **Tension:** Recommendations conflict because they optimize for different goals.
- **Unique findings:** Important issues detected by only one perspective.

Do not treat agreement as proof or resolve disagreements by majority vote.

## Step 5 — Synthesize rather than aggregate

For each important issue, establish:

**Observation → Interpretation → Consequence → Proposed change**

Prioritize by expected impact, confidence in the evidence, implementation effort, and alignment with the central discovery.

Prefer changes that solve several related problems without introducing unnecessary complexity.

Explicitly identify suggestions that should not be implemented.

## Step 6 — Preserve the explanatory purpose

For tools intended to communicate an idea, prioritize:

**Problem → Proposed distinction → Exploration → Discussion**

Interactivity should expose relationships, consequences, or changes in reasoning.

Do not introduce forms, scoring, gamification, or decision automation unless they materially improve that purpose.

## Step 7 — Validate the revised experience

After implementation, check:

- Is the problem immediately recognizable?
- Is the proposed distinction understandable?
- Do the examples demonstrate rather than repeat the idea?
- Does the interaction contribute to understanding?
- Does the discussion add a broader insight?
- Did the revision preserve simplicity and coherence?

When possible, distinguish editorial inspection from evidence gathered through real users.

## Expected review output

Produce a concise report containing:

1. Individual perspective assessments.
2. Shared strengths.
3. Convergent and unique weaknesses.
4. Conflicting recommendations and their resolution.
5. A prioritized shortlist of changes.
6. Changes intentionally rejected or deferred.
7. What still needs validation.

**Core principle:** Different perspectives generate hypotheses about the tool's strengths and weaknesses. The synthesis decides which changes serve the central idea. Validation determines whether those changes actually helped.
