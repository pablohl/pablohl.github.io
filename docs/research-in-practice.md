# Research in Practice editorial guideline

**Purpose:** Help technically capable readers understand what research changes about how they think, build, evaluate or investigate a system. The value is the understanding the reader would otherwise have to construct from the paper, guided by the author’s independent judgment.

The promise is: **understand the finding, see what it changes, and know what evidence is still missing.**

This document owns the core editorial standard. It guides judgment; it does not prescribe a page sequence, a set of visual components or a publication checklist that can replace an interesting idea.

## 1. Editorial principles

### Start with the change in understanding

In the editorial brief, answer explicitly: **What should the reader understand differently after reading this piece?** State the starting intuition and the intended change in understanding; an action recommendation alone does not answer that question. What does this evidence help them see that they could not see before? If that difference is unclear, more explanation or better figures will not establish the piece’s purpose.

Begin with a consequential question and a reason to care. Let the structure follow the evolution of the reader’s understanding, rather than the paper’s order or the latest article’s structure. An anomaly, a single experiment, a misleading metric or an unresolved question can each organize a strong piece.

### Offer an independent interpretation

The author’s judgment is a primary reason for the piece to exist. What is surprising, which assumption is challenged, and why does the distinction matter? Select evidence that develops that interpretation and confront evidence that could weaken it. Accurate reporting and sound evaluation advice support this contribution; they do not replace it.

An interpretation can change how a reader understands a metric, expose a hidden assumption or show why two explanations remain unresolved. Not every finding requires an experiment. Ask what it justifies reconsidering, investigating, measuring or doing differently; develop a bounded test when an empirical choice actually calls for one.

### Preserve evidence integrity

Keep measured findings, the authors’ explanations, personal interpretation and proposed tests distinguishable through plain wording, sources and presentation. Do not imply that association isolates a cause, that a constructed example is an observed outcome, or that a fitted rule transfers beyond its evidence.

Explain the limits that change the conclusion beside the relevant claim. Additional methodological detail can sit at another depth. Labels and layout may reduce repeated caveats, but cannot substitute for an accurate claim.

### Make one coherent, economical experience

The two primary readers share an argument: a CTO or engineer assessing relevance, and a researcher or research-oriented engineer examining explanations and limits. Give both plain language and access to the evidence, with different depths of inspection rather than disconnected audience modes.

Every section, example, figure, qualification and interaction must justify its reading cost. Ask whether the central idea would be harder to understand or assess without it. Correctness and relevance alone do not earn a component its place. Omission is an editorial responsibility; retain material limits, remove duplication and let recurring material develop the idea.

Interaction must reveal a relationship or support a useful investigation. A static explanation is sufficient when it does that work. Buttons, benchmark contrasts, depth sections and selectors are techniques, not defining features of the format.

### Reach a complete, bounded conclusion

Completeness means enough for the chosen question, not coverage of every contribution. The reader should leave able to explain the consequential distinction, the supporting evidence, what remains unknown and what follows from it. Retaining a baseline, changing an interpretation or withholding judgment can be a complete outcome.

## 3. Editorial review and stopping rule

Before publication, assess the piece’s chosen promise:

- **Understanding:** What can the reader now see, question or explain that they could not before?
- **Interpretation:** Is the author’s contribution clear, supported and open to counterevidence?
- **Integrity:** Can the reader identify the evidence and its boundary without reconstructing the paper?
- **Consequence:** What does the insight justify reconsidering, investigating or doing differently? If a test is proposed, is it specific enough to evaluate?
- **Economy and coherence:** What can be removed without weakening the central idea or its evidential support?

Use the [multi-perspective review](multi-perspective-review.md) at a scope proportionate to the change. Where feasible, give one CTO and one research-oriented reader two minutes without coaching. Ask what they understand differently, which evidence supports it and what the paper cannot tell them. If the piece proposes a mechanism, ask what would distinguish it from an alternative. Remembering benchmark names is not the success criterion.

Record **Pass / Needs work / Not checked / Not applicable** with scope and evidence. Editorial perspectives considered by one reviewer are not independent reviews or reader observations. Technical checks establish behavior, not comprehension; unavailable reader evidence is not an automatic publication gate.

**Editorial stopping rule.** An article is ready when the central insight is understandable, the important claims are supported, the relevant limits are clear, and the intended reader can identify what the finding changes. Remaining improvements should address demonstrated confusion, unsupported reasoning, or a meaningful deficiency in the experience—not merely opportunities for additional polish.

A piece need not exhaust the paper, implement every possible interaction, or satisfy every stylistic preference to be complete. The guideline should make editorial judgment more effective, not replace it or prolong revision indefinitely.
