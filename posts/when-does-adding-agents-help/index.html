When does adding agents help?

What one paper found about teams of AI agents—and what its results give us a reason to test.

A reading of Kim et al., Capable language models can outgrow the benefits of collaboration.

My view · executive summary

Start with a capable single agent and see how well it handles your tasks. Before adding agents, decide what you want them to improve: finding information, completing the task correctly, or catching mistakes.

  • Can the work be divided? Try assigning agents different parts of a question—for example, reading different documents—then combining their findings. Compare the final answer with what one agent produces.
  • Is the improvement worth it? Compare answer quality, the money spent on model calls and tools, and how long a user waits for a result.
  • Does another agent catch more mistakes? Compare giving the answer to a second agent to check with asking the original agent to check its own answer. Count new mistakes as well as corrections.

The paper gives reasons to try these comparisons; it does not tell us which will work best in your system. Read my proposed tests ↓

01 / The decision

Should you add agents to a workflow that already works with one?

Here, an agent is a language model working through a task with tools. A team runs several such workers. In a centralized team, a coordinator assigns work, reviews reports and combines the answer.

The same arrangement produced opposite results on two benchmarks. For each task and model, one agent and the team had access to the same tools and the same total allowance for reasoning tokens. The team had to share that allowance across its agents and their coordination.

My view: I would ask where separate work adds useful information, and where handing work between agents makes progress harder. That question connects the results to something we can test in a real workflow.

The contrast suggests an explanation; it does not prove it. Finance Agent and PlanCraft differ in more than their dependencies. Start with the work your agent struggles with, then look for evidence that challenges the proposed explanation.

02 / Explore why

Where is your single agent struggling?

Choose a problem to examine the relevant evidence and a comparison worth running. These are my proposed investigation paths, not predictions about your workflow.

My view / separate investigations

Would separate searches improve the answer?

Evidence: Finance Agent’s centralized team gains 28.2 percentage points over one agent. Separate searches could bring different information into one answer.

Challenge: BrowseComp-Plus also requires finding and combining information across websites. Its centralized mean is almost unchanged (+0.1 points), and its highest team average gains only 2.9 points. Multiple sources alone do not explain the Finance Agent result.

Test: Assign distinct source searches, combine the findings and compare the answer with one agent. Inspect what useful information was added or lost when reports were combined.

Still missing: These benchmark differences do not isolate the effect of dividing searches. Vary that division on the same tasks to test the explanation. Plan the comparison ↓ · Inspect all benchmark averages ↓

My view / shared state and handoffs

Would another agent help execute the next step?

Evidence: In PlanCraft, all four team architectures performed worse on average than a single agent. The tasks require a sequence of actions: each action can change the inventory, affecting what the agent can do next.

Possible explanation: Passing work between agents may make it harder to keep track of these changes. Asking another agent to confirm a recipe or inventory can add a handoff before an action that one agent could perform directly.

Test: Compare one agent with a team that has explicit rules for who changes the inventory and when. Record stale information, repeated actions and failed handoffs alongside task completion.

Still missing: The aggregate result does not establish that handoffs caused the failures, or that better coordination would repair them. Retain one agent if the team adds work without improving completion. Set the acceptance criteria ↓ · Inspect the task definitions ↓

My view / checking answers

Would a second agent catch mistakes the first misses?

Research connection: The paper examines verification and error propagation. That motivates evaluating what checking changes; it does not establish that a reviewer will improve your answers.

Test: Give the same draft answer and evidence either to a second agent or back to the original agent for checking. Use the same criteria and total allowance.

Judge the change: Save the answers before and after checking. Count corrections, missed errors, new errors and correct answers retained. Agreement between agents is insufficient.

Still missing: Whether either check improves final task success enough to justify its cost. Inspect the four checking outcomes ↓

Benchmark figures are published whole-benchmark means across models, not results for the selected workflow. Inspect architecture comparisons and source data ↓

Would a better single agent change the comparison?

The authors find that single-agent performance helps explain when teams gain. That makes the baseline part of the decision: an architecture that helps today may add less after a model upgrade.

My next test: Rerun both one agent and the team when the underlying model changes, using the same tasks and evaluation criteria. Do not assume an earlier team advantage survives. Inspect the capability finding and its limits ↓

Even if multiple agents produce better results, they may require more coordination, model calls and time. The question is whether the improvement justifies those additional costs.

03 / Is it worthwhile?

How much extra work did the teams do?

The success rates leave out the reporting, discussion and checking used to finish. In the 180 configurations with full cost tracking, teams took more reasoning turns on average. The paper counts reasoning–response exchanges as “reasoning turns.” This measures activity, not token use, elapsed time or financial cost. The common budget ceiling is a maximum; runs can finish before using it all.

These averages show extra activity, not a 3.8× or 6.2× token bill. To establish a product cost, measure actual token use, tool charges and elapsed time alongside success. Parallel work may shorten a run while increasing total usage.

Extra work is worthwhile only if it improves the result. That leads to the comparisons I would run next, including whether review catches mistakes.

04 / Run the comparison

What would I test because of these findings?

I would use these findings to choose a comparison, not a default architecture. The tests below ask whether division of work or checking produces a useful gain at an acceptable cost.

1

Test a useful division of work.

If the agent overlooks relevant documents or facts, assign distinct source searches and have agents return sources and findings to a coordinator. Compare with one agent on fresh, predefined tasks. Check whether additional evidence improves the answer; vary the division within the same task set to investigate why. If the combined answer is no better, wider search coverage alone is not a reason to keep the team.

2

Measure the improvement and its price together.

Keep the total budget ceiling aligned and record success, actual tokens, tool charges and time. Before running the comparison, specify how much better the answer must be and how much extra spending and waiting you can accept. Reject a team that exceeds those limits even if its success rate improves. If you raise the team’s allowance, also test one agent with the larger budget.

3

Compare a reviewer with a self-check.

Compare a second agent checking the draft answer with the original agent checking its own answer, using the same evidence, criteria and total ceiling. A coordinator can review reports and peers can challenge findings, but agreement alone does not establish correctness. Keep a separate reviewer only if it improves final task results enough to justify the extra work, including any new errors it introduces.

Run each approach multiple times to see how much the results vary, and measure whether the improvement is large enough to justify the added cost. The paper justifies trying these comparisons; it does not settle their outcome for your system. If the gain is insufficient, improve or retain the single-agent baseline.

05 / Examine the research

What supports this reading—and where does it stop?

The study covers 260 combinations of model, benchmark and architecture, using models from OpenAI, Google and Anthropic. The full task inventory and comparisons below let you challenge the opening contrast. These are the study’s models, not current recommendations.

Research question: Does splitting work improve results because it adds useful evidence, or because it changes instructions, attempts or checking? Hold the task set and model fixed while varying those conditions; a difference between benchmarks cannot settle that explanation.

Baseline capability: a reason to retest, not a cutoff to deploy.

The researchers found that adding agents was less likely to help when a single agent was already performing well. They estimated a threshold of roughly 45% single-agent success: above this level, their rule predicts that adding agents will provide no improvement or make performance worse.

In a separate validation on SWE-bench Verified and Terminal-Bench, the rule correctly predicted whether teams would help or hurt in 15 of 16 model-and-benchmark combinations (reported as 94%). Each combination was evaluated on only 20 tasks, so the result is too limited to treat 45% as a general rule for other workflows. Paper: capability and validation ↗

Statistical qualification and transfer to other benchmarks

The interaction between baseline performance and team size did not survive cluster-robust correction, a check that accounts for similarities among observations from the same benchmark. The authors therefore present the threshold as a fitted selection rule, not a universal scaling law. Their separate performance predictor also transfers poorly to a held-out benchmark (mean leave-one-dataset-out R² = −2.09). Together, these limits support retesting the comparison on your tasks rather than adopting the threshold as a rule.

Which teams were compared?

For each benchmark, the study compares one agent with four team architectures. The overview selects the lowest and highest architecture means; the chart then lets you inspect each arrangement.

Independent
Agents work separately without exchanging messages. The system then combines their answers, without the agents reviewing or discussing each other’s answers.
Centralized
A coordinator assigns work, reviews reports and combines the answer.
Decentralized
Workers share and discuss findings with each other before selecting an answer.
Hybrid
A coordinator directs work, and workers can also exchange findings.
