Every Explanation Has a Cost

Imagine explaining a software platform to a prospective client.

You describe its main capabilities, demonstrate how they work, and give a few examples. The listener follows along and understands what each feature does.

But afterward, they struggle to see how the platform would fit into their own work.

The explanation was clear. Nothing you said was necessarily wrong. Yet something important was missing.

I have been thinking about this while learning to communicate technical ideas to people with different backgrounds. One recurring suggestion is to make explanations simpler: use concrete examples, avoid unfamiliar terminology, and make every step easy to follow.

That is generally good advice. But it raises a question: what kind of thinking should an explanation ask of its listener?

Where does the work go?

Explaining something is itself a decision: what information to include, what to leave out, and what understanding the listener actually needs.

Consider three ways to explain a complicated system.

You can provide too little context, leaving the listener to reconstruct why things matter and how they connect.

You can explain everything, leaving the listener to sort through the details and identify what is important.

Or you can organize the information around a particular purpose, explaining how the pieces fit together while leaving the listener to consider where they could use them.

The third approach sounds preferable. But it assumes we know what the listener needs.

Suppose a platform can retrieve information from documents and use it across different business workflows.

Someone asks whether it can find specific answers in a long PDF. You demonstrate the retrieval capability, explain how it works, and show the results. Your explanation answers their question.

But during the conversation, they mention that finding information is rarely their biggest problem. The difficulty comes afterward, when that information needs to be verified, shared with another team, and approved.

The conversation shifts from finding information to reviewing it and coordinating approvals.

The original explanation wasn't wrong. It answered a question that turned out to be less important than either person initially realized.

Sometimes we make listeners work because we haven't explained ourselves well. They must reconstruct missing context, guess our assumptions, or identify what matters among unnecessary details.

Other times, their effort is productive. They connect an idea to their own experience, recognize implications, or discover a question they hadn't considered.

Making someone work to understand what we meant is different from giving them something worth thinking about.

Understanding develops together

The speaker knows the subject. The listener knows their own situation. Neither necessarily knows which connections will matter before the conversation begins.

We cannot always prepare the right explanation in advance. Sometimes the conversation helps the listener discover what they need to know and helps the speaker identify what is worth explaining.

This relates to Relevance Theory, developed by Dan Sperber and Deirdre Wilson. The theory considers both how information changes someone's understanding and how much mental effort it takes to interpret it. Some explanations demand more effort because the understanding they offer is worth that effort.

But reducing effort can also introduce risks. For example, telling a client that their data is secure because a major cloud provider hosts it may sound reassuring, while leaving out important questions about access controls and responsibility. Making an explanation simpler should not make it misleading.

Returning to the software demonstration, the client understood how the platform retrieves information. But the conversation revealed that finding information wasn't their main problem. What mattered was what happened afterward.

Explaining retrieval in greater detail wouldn't necessarily have helped. The important connection emerged when the client began relating the demonstration to their own work.

An explanation doesn't eliminate the work of understanding. It changes who performs that work and what kind of work remains.
