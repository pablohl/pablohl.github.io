When Doing It Myself Gets Cheaper

AI made a familiar task quicker to do myself. That left me wondering what I would be giving up by keeping it.

I recently completed a task with AI that would be suitable for an intern.

I understood the context and the main problem. I wasn’t sure of the solution, but I had a clear goal. With recent LLM models, I could investigate the code, make changes, and test them without doing the implementation myself. I was still deciding what to try and whether the results were good enough.

That made me reconsider what I would gain by delegating the task. If an intern did the implementation while I continued making those decisions, I might spend just as much time on it. But part of what I want to help them learn is how to make those decisions themselves: to bring an approach, explain why they chose it, and show what they checked. I want to review their reasoning instead of supplying it. That takes time together, even when doing the current task myself would be quicker.

AI changes the alternative to delegation

Delegating would have meant explaining the problem, transferring context, answering questions, and reviewing the result.

AI can make an intern faster while reducing the time I save by delegating. That happens when it saves me more time doing the work than supporting them.

AI can reduce supervision too: the engineer may use it to find context, investigate alternatives, and catch mistakes before review. The question is which side of the comparison gets cheaper faster.

For example:

Only the senior engineer’s hours are counted. This comparison says nothing about how much faster the junior engineer works, or how often this reversal happens.

Here, both options get faster, but delegating no longer saves time.

Doing it myself may save me time today. But if I keep choosing the approach and deciding what counts as enough evidence, the other person gets less practice doing either. I may finish this task faster while leaving the next one just as dependent on me.

The work can get done, and someone can learn from it, while I still need to direct the next task. That made me wonder when an assignment actually starts saving the person delegating it time.

The same task, four settings

Imagine a task: investigate, implement, and test an integration with an external service. The four situations below all start with that task, and everyone has AI. I’m changing who helps the engineer, how long they work together, and who decides what to do next.

01A startup intern, for ten weeks

The CTO could finish the integration with AI in six hours. Helping the intern takes eight. The integration works, but the CTO has spent two extra hours. With a ten-week internship, there are fewer future assignments where that learning can save the CTO time.

02The same startup, a permanent hire

The first integration costs just as much attention. On the next one, the CTO asks the engineer to propose the approach and explain the tests, then reviews their reasoning. Repeated assignments give them context; being trusted to choose an approach gives them practice making decisions. Hiring them for longer doesn’t make that happen on its own.

03A company intern, for ten weeks

A working example, tests, and a mentor make the first integration easier to start. The mentor still spends time reviewing it, but does not have to explain the system from scratch. Those existing resources, rather than the company’s size itself, make the short engagement less costly.

04The same company, a permanent hire

The lead keeps breaking every problem into steps and choosing what to investigate. The engineer gets faster with AI, but has little reason or room to propose the next step. Six months of completed tasks need not change that relationship. The dependency here is partly in how the lead assigns the work.

Who is directing the investigation?

A task can be completed correctly while the lead still chooses what to investigate, what to try, and what evidence is enough. The work has moved to someone else; directing it has not.

I may still review every significant decision, especially when the consequences demand it. That does not tell me whether delegation is working. What matters is whether I am examining someone’s reasoning or having to formulate it for them. I can remain accountable in either case.

Knowing when to ask for help is part of that judgment. Fewer questions are useful only when the person can recognize what needs escalation.

A review I remember

In one review, an intern brought examples of incorrect answers that our matching rule had counted as correct. That made our earlier accuracy results look less reliable. I asked what that meant for our evaluation and which earlier conclusions we needed to revisit.

We had evidence to discuss, but I was still helping work out what to do with it. What I wanted them to bring next time was that next step too, the consequence, the “so what?”: what they thought the findings changed, and why.

I would still expect questions, reviews, and disagreements. The change I am looking for is having an approach to discuss, rather than having to supply one. That can happen in one familiar part of a system long before it happens elsewhere.

What I would do differently

What we need to consider is how easily I could make the same decision on the next task, and the next, because each looks cheaper to do myself.

I would now ask what I am leaving for someone else to learn to decide. If I keep the unfamiliar problems and delegate only the steps I have already worked out, I should not be surprised when I am still needed to work out the next one.

That means sometimes accepting a slower assignment and reviewing an approach I could have chosen myself. It also means using AI to help the other engineer find context, test ideas, and catch mistakes before we talk. Whether that saves me time sooner is something to observe, not assume.
