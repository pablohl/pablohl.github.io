An interactive experiment

Where would you put your stand?

Three vendors sell the same ice cream at the same price. Each wants as many customers as possible. Move the stands around the circular beach and see who the customers choose.

Inspired by the Lemonade Stand Game: lemonade in the original, ice cream here. This is a simplified adaptation; the first two views allow free movement, while the third uses twelve locations and repeated rounds.

View 1 · Evenly spread: We represent a uniform customer distribution with 100 equally spaced customers around the shore.

View 2 · Crowded stretch: Sixty customers are spread over one quarter of the shore; the remaining forty occupy the other three quarters.

View 3 · Next day: Customers are evenly spread again. Vendors choose among twelve locations each day, using only what they saw the day before.

Drag a stand around the shore, or use the sliders. The other stands stay where you leave them.

The shaded arc is the crowded quarter. Its three rows of dots show the higher density. Stand positions stay the same when you switch views.

Choose A’s next location by dragging or using its slider. The twelve pale dots are available spots.

Customer share

Each customer goes to the nearest stand along the shore. If two stands are equally close, they split that customer.

If B and C stay where they are, A would serve — of customers.

But B and C will choose too. Compared with that estimate, what share will A actually get?

Choose a prediction to reveal the next day. “Not sure” is fine.

How do B and C choose?

Each independently picks the spot that would have served them best if yesterday’s other stands stayed put. They cannot see your new choice or each other’s new choice. If spots tie, they prefer the one closest to yesterday’s spot.

Days played

    What this shows—and what it doesn’t

    In this small model, the objective and customer behavior are known: all three vendors want more customers, and customers choose by distance. Moving a stand changes its share, but the other vendors do not respond automatically. The third view adds another day of simultaneous choices.

    The second view changes only where customers are. The shaded quarter is an illustrative scenario, not a claim about a real beach.

    Here, B and C follow one deliberately simple rule based on the previous day. Their moves are simultaneous with yours, not instant responses to it. This shows what changes when the other vendors move, but it does not claim that real people—or the original game’s agents—follow this rule.
