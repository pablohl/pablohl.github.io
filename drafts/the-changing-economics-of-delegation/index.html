The Changing Economics of Delegation

AI can make a junior engineer faster while reducing the time a senior saves by delegating. Developing independent ownership is a different investment.

I recently completed a task with AI that would be suitable for an intern.

I understood the context and the main problem. I wasn’t sure of the solution, but I had a clear goal. The work involved investigating code, making changes, testing, and reviewing results. With recent LLM models, I could focus on the decisions without doing any of the implementation myself.

AI changes the alternative to delegation

Delegating would have meant explaining the problem, transferring context, answering questions, and reviewing the result. The relevant comparison was how much of my attention that would require versus doing the work directly with AI.

AI can make a junior engineer more productive while making the same task less worth delegating. Their implementation gets faster, but so does mine.

AI can reduce supervision too: the engineer may use it to find context, investigate alternatives, and catch mistakes before review. The question is which side of the comparison gets cheaper faster.

Illustrative numbers for one task. Only the senior engineer’s hours are counted; the junior engineer’s own working time is excluded.

Both options need less of the senior engineer’s time. Yet delegation changes from saving four hours to requiring one extra hour, because doing the work directly becomes cheaper faster.

That creates a tension for a technical lead. Doing the work myself may be efficient today. Repeatedly making that choice also removes opportunities for someone else to develop the judgment that would make delegation useful later.

When working with interns, I have invested time in helping them become independent enough to take responsibility for increasingly complex problems. That is a different objective from finishing the current task with the least supervision.

A useful deliverable can justify the assignment without establishing that anyone else has gained capacity. The distinction becomes clearer across four situations.

Similar work, different economics

Consider four hypothetical situations. In each, the task is the same: someone investigates, implements, and validates an integration with an external service. AI is available to everyone, including the person delegating.

01A two-person startup hires an intern for ten weeks.

The CTO understands the architecture and requirements. With AI, the integration would take six hours to implement and validate. The intern also uses AI, but needs onboarding, explanations, and reviews. Across the assignment, the CTO spends eight hours helping them complete it.

The integration works and the intern learns. But this assignment has consumed two more hours of the CTO’s attention than doing it directly.

CTO spends 8 h; doing it directly takes 6 h.
Duration
Ten weeks to recover the initial effort.
Responsibility
Needs explanations and review.

02The same startup hires a junior engineer for the long term.

The first integration is similarly expensive. Several months later, though, the engineer understands the product, recognizes common problems, and investigates new integrations with limited supervision. The early investment begins to create capacity.

Attention
Substantial guidance at the start.
Duration
Months to build context and independence.
Responsibility
Investigates new integrations with less supervision.

03A 50-person company brings in an intern for ten weeks.

Documentation, integration patterns, automated tests, and a designated mentor are already in place. They reduce the effort needed to get the intern started on a bounded integration.

The mentor’s time still counts. The difference is that each new assignment does not require the CTO to reconstruct all the context.

Attention
Established patterns reduce setup; the mentor still spends time.
Duration
Ten weeks, with support already in place.
Responsibility
Owns a bounded integration; key decisions are reviewed.

04The same company hires a junior engineer permanently.

The engineer uses AI effectively and produces good technical work. But six months later, the technical lead still defines every task, resolves most ambiguities, and reviews every significant decision.

The company has added execution capacity. It may have gained less independence than it expected.

Attention
Lead still resolves ambiguities and reviews decisions.
Duration
Permanent role; dependency persists after six months.
Responsibility
Completes tasks; the lead still directs the work.

Who is directing the investigation?

An integration can be implemented correctly while the lead still chooses the failure modes to investigate, the experiments to run, and the evidence needed to accept a solution. The code has moved to someone else; directing the work has not.

I may remain accountable for the outcome after delegating. The capacity gain comes from someone else leading the investigation within agreed boundaries, rather than needing me to supply each decision.

Knowing when to ask for help is part of that judgment. Fewer questions are useful only when the person can recognize what needs escalation.

Teaching the reasoning costs more at first

With interns, I initially defined experiments, explained the methodology, and reviewed results. Later, I gave the problem, constraints, and what we needed to learn. The next step was for them to propose experiments, choose metrics and stopping conditions, and explain how the evidence would inform a decision.

Reviewing those choices could take more effort than making them myself. But supplying the answer each time would preserve the dependency. My part was to provide context, room to make decisions, and clear escalation boundaries—not simply less detailed instructions.

This progression is specific to an area of work. Owning an integration does not imply readiness to own the product, and faster AI-assisted implementation does not establish either.

The time available changes the return

Suppose onboarding takes 20 hours of a senior engineer’s attention, after which the new employee saves them three hours a week, net of supervision. Four productive weeks recover 12 hours; 40 weeks recover 120. The same initial effort leaves the senior eight hours behind in the first case and 100 hours ahead in the second.

The company might choose the first tradeoff to develop someone it hopes to hire later. That can be intentional, but it has not provided extra capacity during those four weeks.

Explore the assumptions

The model compares time the existing team avoids spending on the work with time it spends onboarding and supervising. The alternative is the team doing the work directly with AI.

Existing team’s net time gained = work avoided − onboarding − supervision

Choose a scenario and adjust the assumptions. In particular, compare continued supervision with a gradual decrease in the direction the person needs.

The model shows the consequences of the assumptions you choose. It cannot tell you how quickly a person will become independent. That judgment has to come from the work: what they can investigate, validate, and recommend with less direction.

Choose which investment you are making

The risk is turning “I can do this faster with AI” into a permanent rule for assigning work. It can keep delivery moving while leaving every unfamiliar problem dependent on the same senior engineer.

I would distinguish work chosen for immediate delivery from work chosen to develop ownership. On the latter, a slower assignment can be worthwhile if it changes who does the reasoning next time. That takes real decision-making authority and support from the lead; it also takes enough time and money for the investment to develop.

I would look for a person who can frame the next investigation, challenge an AI-generated answer, and return with evidence and a recommendation I can trust. That is a change in our working relationship that faster implementation alone cannot provide.

The return on that investment is being able to review the reasoning instead of supplying it.
