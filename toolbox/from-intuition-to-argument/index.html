From Intuition to Argument

What exactly is my intuition noticing, and why does it matter to this decision?

Use this when something feels wrong, but you cannot yet explain the concern clearly.

You’ll be able to turn a concern into an explicit claim, supporting reasons, and a check others can examine.

The idea

Intuition can be compressed experience, but it is a private signal. Turning it into explicit reasoning tests the intuition before asking others to accept it.

Worked example · illustrative

A trip plan that feels too tight

Situation. A friend proposes an itinerary with a short connection between a train and the last ferry to an island. You have made similar trips and feel uneasy, but cannot initially explain why.

The tempting conclusion. I have traveled more, so we should reject this itinerary.

What changes the view. Checking the route reveals a bus transfer between the station and ferry terminal. Your concern becomes specific: a modest train delay could leave too little transfer time, with no later ferry. If the bus is coordinated with the train or another crossing exists, the concern may weaken. Experience suggested a question; the timetable lets everyone inspect it.

What this supports. Check transfer times and the consequence of missing the ferry, then choose whether an earlier train is worth the extra time. Replace “it feels risky” with the actual failure path. Withdraw the objection if the connection is adequately protected.

Useful when

Explaining a concernReviewing a proposalDiscussing a disagreementChecking an experienced first reaction

Watch for

Don't use reasoning merely to rationalize the initial reaction. Investigation may show that the intuition was wrong.
