What Did the Metric Throw Away?

Could information removed by this metric change the decision?

Use this when a metric appears to give a clear answer, but observations or experience don't quite agree.

You’ll be able to identify an omitted distinction that could change the choice behind a headline metric.

The idea

Metrics are compression. They can be perfectly correct while excluding distinctions that matter to the actual decision.

Worked example · illustrative

Two commutes with the same average

Situation. You need to reach childcare pickup by six. Two routes average 30 minutes. One usually takes 25–35 minutes; the other often takes 15 minutes but sometimes takes an hour. You must choose a route and departure time.

The tempting conclusion. The average is the same, so either route is equally suitable.

What changes the view. Getting there early most days doesn't help on the day you miss pickup. Both routes average 30 minutes, but one can occasionally take an hour. What matters here is how often each route would get you there after six, given when you usually leave.

What this supports. For a pickup by six, the more predictable route may be a better fit, even though its average is no faster. On a day without a deadline, you might prefer the route that's often quicker. The same average can hide a difference that matters for one trip and less for another.

Useful when

Comparing averagesChoosing between servicesReviewing rankingsInterpreting performance measures

Watch for

Don't respond by measuring everything. Missing information matters only if it could alter the decision.
