Connect Work to Consequences

What feedback would help me understand what happened because of my work?

The idea

Evaluating the work itself and understanding its consequences provide different information. Learning requires enough visibility into downstream use, difficulties, and outcomes to investigate how the contribution mattered.

Worked example · illustrative

An event that few people attended

Situation. You organized a community event carefully, but attendance was low. Feedback says the arrangements were clear. You still do not know whether your work was useful or what to change next time.

The tempting conclusion. Low attendance means the organizing was poor, so improve the invitations and logistics.

What changes the view. Conversations with invitees reveal that many wanted to come but could not manage the time. Those who attended describe helpful introductions, and two later arrange a shared project. These observations separate the quality of preparation, barriers to attendance, and consequences of attending. They do not establish that your organizing alone caused the later collaboration.

What this supports. Follow up on both participation barriers and what attendees gained before choosing what to improve. A different time may matter more than more polished invitations. Treat outcomes as evidence to investigate alongside your decisions and external circumstances, rather than as a simple score for your contribution.

Situation

You can assess the technical quality of your work but cannot tell whether it helped, or what you should improve.

Useful when

Reviewing applied researchMentoring someone through a projectEvaluating a technical investmentUnderstanding why a feature did not helpPlanning feedback after a launch

Watch for

Outcomes alone do not establish the quality of a decision or an individual's contribution. Other people, external conditions, chance, and delayed effects can influence what happens.
