Engineering Toolbox / Hiring

What Did the Interview Actually Tell You?

An interview observes behavior. Hiring predicts future work. What connects the two?

01 / The problem

From an interview moment to a prediction

A technical interview gives us a limited view of how someone thinks. We ask questions, introduce constraints, and observe how they respond.

But hiring requires a different judgment: what do we expect this person to contribute on real problems, with the support and constraints of our team?

It’s easy to move from an encouraging interview moment to a broad conclusion about someone’s potential without examining the assumptions in between.

02 / The proposed distinction

Separate three parts of the reasoning:

Evidence →then Expectation →then Uncertainty

Evidence
What did we observe?
Expectation
What work might they handle, with what support?
Uncertainty
What remains untested, and how would we check it?

A strong observation can support a reasonable expectation without proving it. The examples below explore where that boundary falls.

03 / Explore an example

What does the evidence support?

Two illustrative interview scenarios. Explore either one, then continue to the discussion.

Example 01 / ML evaluation

They spotted a problem. Did it change their recommendation?

You’re hiring an ML engineer who will help decide whether models are ready to ship. In the interview, you show a classifier with 92% accuracy and ask whether the team should deploy it. The candidate says there could be data leakage: information available during testing might not be available when the model is used.

The tempting conclusion

“They spotted leakage. They have good evaluation judgment.”

That could be true. But naming a familiar problem and knowing what to do about it are different pieces of evidence.

Look for what the concern changes

A concern becomes more useful evidence when it changes the proposed test, the recommendation, or the next action. Saying “there may be leakage” tells you less than showing how that possibility changes the decision.

How this could sound in the hiring discussion
“In this case, they turned an evaluation risk into a concrete check before release. I’d expect them to contribute to model reviews, with help on our domain’s metrics. I haven’t yet seen whether they do the same with an unfamiliar problem.”

A useful follow-up: Give them a different evaluation scenario and ask what would make them change their recommendation. Look for whether they adapt the reasoning rather than repeat the same test.

Example 02 / System design

They don’t know our queue. What does that tell us?

You’re hiring a backend engineer to work on a service that uses a message queue. The candidate hasn’t used your queue product. You still need to understand whether they can reason about failures, repeated messages, and recovery.

The tempting conclusion

“They don’t know our stack. They’ll struggle with reliability.”

Unfamiliarity with a product is visible. Their ability to reason about reliability needs its own evidence.

“We didn’t see it” and “they struggled with it” are different

An interview that never tested failure handling leaves a question open. An answer that overlooks a failure after it has been explained gives you something concrete to examine. Neither warrants a broad claim about every aspect of the candidate’s ability.

How this could sound in the hiring discussion
“They’ll need to learn our queue. Before judging their reliability skills, I’d discuss a duplicate-message scenario and ask how they would prevent repeated side effects. Product experience and failure reasoning are separate questions.”

A useful follow-up: Explain the delivery behavior without requiring product-specific vocabulary. Ask them to trace one message through a failure and recovery. Then assess the reasoning against the role’s expectations.

04 / Discussion

What this distinction helps us notice

A concern matters through its consequences

The ML comparison separates recognizing a familiar risk from using it to change a decision. That helps us ask what the candidate’s reasoning would change in the work, rather than how convincing the answer sounded.

A technical gap needs a more specific question

The queue comparison separates product knowledge from reasoning about failures. Before calling a gap a weakness, ask whether the interview tested the capability the role needs. Missing evidence and an observed difficulty call for different follow-ups.

An expectation still depends on context

Neither example shows how the person will perform across unfamiliar problems, team constraints, or different levels of support. A strong observation can support a reasonable expectation without proving it. Naming those conditions makes the prediction easier to question and revisit.

Turn the evidence into a hiring expectation

The three parts keep the observation, the prediction, and its limits separate. Here is the ML example brought together.

A sentence to take with you

We observed [specific behavior]. That gives us reason to expect [work contribution], with [support]. We’re still unsure about [gap], which we could check by [next step].

You can use this in your existing notes. If a part is missing, name the gap rather than filling it with a broad impression.

Check the standard, too. If you call a gap “learnable” for one candidate and “disqualifying” for another, explain which requirement of the role justifies the difference.
