Engineering guides / Technical decisions

What Does This Result Let Us Decide?

Use this when a technical result looks promising, but the next action is unclear.

You’ll be able to state what the result supports, the conditions on your recommendation, and what still needs checking.

The distinction

A result answers a question. A decision depends on what the next step requires.

A promising result is not yet a recommendation. Ask what it establishes, what it leaves open, and whether that gap matters for the action you want to take.

Observation →then Interpretation →then Decision

01 / Follow a decision

Start with a situation

Choose a situation. Within each story, change a condition to compare what the evidence supports.

Two fictional situations. Explore the detail that changes each decision.

Fewer interruptions. Would we still notice when customers need help?

An online shop sends an urgent notification to the engineer responsible for fixing problems when purchases start failing. The point is to get someone’s attention so they can investigate and help customers buy again. But short-lived problems also trigger alerts, waking the engineer even when the problem has already passed.

The team received 18 overnight alerts last week. Someone proposes making the rule less sensitive, so it waits for failures to become more common before notifying anyone. Applied to last week’s activity, it would have sent only four alerts.

The proposal

“The less sensitive rule would have sent four alerts instead of eighteen last week. Let’s adopt it.”

Fewer interruptions would help the engineer. But the rule still needs to call someone in when needed.

What has the team checked?

What this establishes Changed

Next step Changed

Why this follows

The count is lower. The incidents behind it are still unexamined.

Nobody has reviewed what happened after each alert: what kind of incident triggered it, how serious it was, or whether someone had to intervene.

How to proceed: Before changing the rule, review the incidents behind the alerts it would remove.

How this could sound in the report
“The less sensitive rule would have sent four alerts instead of eighteen last week. We have not reviewed the incidents behind the alerts it removes. I recommend checking their severity and whether they needed intervention before adopting the rule.”

A useful follow-up: For each removed alert, review the incident and the team’s response. Record which incidents needed intervention and whether the proposed rule would still have alerted someone.

The team reviewed the incidents behind the alerts. One involved customers being unable to buy and required the engineer to intervene.

It reduces the alert count, but would also miss an incident that needed intervention.

How this could sound in the report
“The less sensitive rule would have sent four alerts instead of eighteen last week, but it would remove an alert for a purchase failure that needed intervention. The current rule alerted the engineer; the proposed rule would not. I recommend revising the rule before adopting it.”

A useful follow-up: Revise which incidents trigger an alert. Recheck the revised rule against incidents that needed intervention and those that did not.

The missing connection

Eighteen alerts becoming four tells us about interruptions. Reviewing the incidents tells us what those alerts were for. That connection determines whether fewer alerts supports adopting this rule.

Active orders passed the trial. Does the historical archive have to move too?

An online shop is preparing to move its order records to a new database—the system that stores them. Active orders are the orders staff still handle day to day. The historical archive holds completed orders kept for reference, using an older storage format.

In a practice run, the active orders moved correctly: amounts matched, and staff could find and read them. The historical archive was not tested. A colleague says, “Then the whole move has to wait.”

The proposal

“The historical archive hasn’t been tested. We can’t move the active orders either.”

Can the archive remain accessible in the old system while staff use active orders in the new one? Or does the shop need both together in the new system?

Must the historical archive move with the active orders?

Select a condition. Evidence and next step below will update.

Unchanged: Active orders passed the trial. The historical archive is still untested.

Evidence and next step

Showing: No—the archive can stay in the old system

What this establishes Changed
The archive can stay accessible in the old system; the limited arrangement has been checked.
Next step Changed
Move the active orders. Keep the archive where it is.
Why this follows

The active orders can move while the archive waits.

Staff can find the right records across both systems, and the team has checked that the active orders stay correct and that the limited move can be undone safely.

How to proceed: Move active orders in small groups and check that they remain accessible and correct. Leave the archive in the old system until its own trial succeeds.

How this could sound in the report
“Active orders passed the trial. We have checked that staff can use them in the new system while the historical archive stays accessible in the old one, and that the limited move can be undone safely. I recommend moving the active orders while we test the archive separately.”

A useful follow-up: Check that staff can still find the right records during the limited move. Test the archive’s older format before including it.

Showing: Yes—staff need the active orders and archive together

What this establishes Changed
Staff need the archive in the new system too, but its older format has not been tested.
Next step Changed
Wait to move either group. Test the archive first.
Why this follows

The archive must be tested before this move.

The new customer-support screen needs active orders and purchase history together. Staff cannot use it with the archive left in the old system. The active-order trial does not establish that the archive’s older format will move correctly.

How to proceed: Hold the complete move. Test the archive’s older format, fix any problems, and check that the complete move can be undone safely before setting a date.

How this could sound in the report
“Active orders passed the trial, but staff need the historical archive in the new system too. Its older format has not been tested, so I recommend holding the complete move until that trial succeeds and a safe way to undo the move has been checked.”

A useful follow-up: Test the archive’s older format and fix any problems. Verify the complete move and a safe way to undo it before setting a date.

The missing connection

If the archive can stay accessible in the old system, its missing test does not prevent the checked move of active orders. If staff need both in the new system, test the archive before moving either group.

02 / Take the idea with you

Name what the gap changes.

Reviewing the incidents behind the alerts can show that a quieter rule would miss a problem needing intervention. The archive can stay in the old system if staff can still do their work with active orders in the new one. Explain which requirement connects the result to your recommended action.

Wording to take with you

We found [result] when we tested [cases]. This supports [interpretation], but leaves [gap]. Because the next step needs [requirement], I recommend [action and scope].
