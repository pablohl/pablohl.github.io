Engineering Toolbox / Technical decisions

What Does This Result Let Us Decide?

Fewer overnight pages. A successful migration rehearsal. Both sound promising. What would you recommend next?

The numbers look good. The next step is less clear.

A promising result arrives with a caveat. Is it a reason to stop, a reason to narrow the plan, or something to check before going further?

Follow two engineering decisions. In each one, change what the team knows or needs—and see why the recommendation changes.

01 / Follow a decision

Start with a situation

Two fictional situations. Explore the detail that changes each decision.

Eighteen pages. Four worth waking up for?

It is 2 a.m. The on-call engineer gets another page from the checkout service. More than 1% of requests have timed out for five minutes. By the time they open the dashboard, the spike has passed.

The team has received 18 timeout pages in a week. Someone proposes raising the timeout threshold to 5%. Applied to last week’s traffic, that rule would have sent just four pages.

The proposal

“Four pages instead of eighteen. Let’s raise the threshold.”

That would mean fewer interrupted nights. But would the new rule still wake someone when customers cannot complete a purchase?

What has the team checked?

A quieter night is promising. Detection is still an open question.

The new rule would have sent four pages last week. Nobody has checked whether the pages it removes include incidents that needed a response.

Keep the current rule for now. Replay known outages against the proposed threshold and check whether it would alert soon enough for the team to respond.

The useful next check is about what the rule would miss. Another count of pages cannot answer that.

The quieter rule would have missed a real outage.

On Thursday, 3% of checkout requests timed out long enough for customers to lose purchases. The current rule paged the engineer. A 5% threshold would not have paged at all.

Do not adopt this threshold. Keep the current rule while looking for a way to filter brief spikes without hiding sustained checkout failures, then replay known incidents again.

Fewer pages was a real improvement in one measure. The replay shows why that improvement does not justify this change.

The missing connection

Fewer pages tells us about interruptions. To recommend this rule, we also need to know what happens to incident detection. Writing the recommendation exposes the part the first measurement did not answer.

How this could sound in the report
“The proposed threshold would reduce last week’s pages from 18 to 4, but it would also miss Thursday’s checkout outage. I recommend keeping the current rule while we revise how it filters brief spikes. The quieter rule does not meet our need to detect sustained customer failures.”

A useful follow-up: Find a revised rule that distinguishes brief spikes from sustained failures. Replay known incidents and inspect detection time before changing the production alert.

The rehearsal passed. Why is the migration still on hold?

The team has spent weeks preparing to move its order database. In the rehearsal, recent orders arrive intact: totals match, relationships hold, and the application reads them correctly. The team wants to schedule the move.

Then someone asks about the archive. Older orders use a different schema, and none were included in the rehearsal. A colleague says the migration should stop until every historical format has been tested.

The proposal

“We haven’t tested the archive. We can’t move anything yet.”

The gap is real. But does the archive have to move this weekend, or can it stay where it is while recent orders move first?

What does the next move actually require?

The archive can wait. A limited move has support.

The recent-order rehearsal passed. The team has also verified that the application can access orders across both databases consistently and that it can roll back. Historical formats remain untested.

Move the tested recent orders in stages and monitor the agreed checks. Keep the archive in the old database until its formats have been rehearsed.

The gap limits which records can move. It does not, by itself, rule out the smaller move the team can safely support.

This move needs evidence the rehearsal did not provide.

The recent-order rehearsal still passes. But the application cannot operate with the archive left behind, and historical formats remain untested.

Hold the full migration. Rehearse representative historical formats, resolve failures, and verify the checks and rollback plan before scheduling the move.

The same caveat now concerns records required for the next step. It blocks this plan, even though the recent-order result remains valid.

The missing connection

“The archive is untested” describes a limit of the rehearsal. Its consequence depends on the plan: it may restrict the move to recent orders, or prevent the full move from going ahead.

02 / Take the idea with you

What made the recommendations change?

The alert count and the migration rehearsal each gave a useful answer. Neither answered every question needed for the next step. A clear recommendation makes that connection visible.

Name the decision criterion.

The on-call team wants fewer unnecessary interruptions while still detecting customer problems. Counting pages answers only part of that question.

Make the caveat change something.

The migration rehearsal does not cover the archive. Whether that stops the next move depends on whether the archive has to move with it.

Identify the work that remains.

An unclear result needs analysis. An unclear connection needs explanation. An untested assumption needs evidence. A disputed trade-off needs a decision from the responsible person.

A sentence to take with you

We found [result] on [cases]. That supports [interpretation], but leaves [gap]. Given [what the next step requires], I recommend [action and scope].

If a part is missing, decide whether you need analysis, an explanation, evidence, or agreement on a trade-off.

Keep the conclusion proportionate. A recommendation can be to continue, narrow the scope, retain the current approach, or collect specific evidence. State what the uncertainty permits or prevents.
