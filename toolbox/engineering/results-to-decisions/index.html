Engineering Toolbox / Technical decisions

What Does This Result Let Us Decide?

A quieter night. A successful trial run. What do these results actually let a team do next?

The distinction

A result answers a question. A decision depends on what the next step requires.

A promising result is not yet a recommendation. Ask what it establishes, what it leaves open, and whether that gap matters for the action you want to take.

Observation →then Interpretation →then Decision

01 / Follow a decision

Start with a situation

Two fictional situations. Explore the detail that changes each decision.

Fewer interruptions. Would we still notice when customers need help?

An online shop sends an urgent notification to the engineer responsible for fixing problems when purchases start failing. The point is to get someone’s attention so they can investigate and help customers buy again. But short-lived problems also trigger alerts, waking the engineer even when the problem has already passed.

The team received 18 overnight alerts last week. Someone proposes making the rule less sensitive, so it waits for failures to become more common before notifying anyone. Applied to last week’s activity, it would have sent only four alerts.

The proposal

“Four alerts instead of eighteen. Let’s change the rule.”

Fewer interruptions would help the engineer. But the rule still needs to call someone in when customers cannot buy. Has the team checked whether it would do that?

What has the team checked?

Fewer interruptions. We still don’t know what would be missed.

The proposed rule would have sent four alerts last week. Nobody has checked whether the removed alerts include customer problems that needed attention.

Keep the current rule while checking how the proposed rule would have behaved during known purchase failures.

The next check must ask whether someone would still be notified in time to help. Counting alerts again cannot answer that.

How this could sound in the report
“The proposed rule would reduce last week’s alerts from 18 to 4. We have not checked which customer problems it would miss. I recommend testing it against known purchase failures before changing the rule: fewer interruptions only help if someone is still notified when help is needed.”

A useful follow-up: Use records of past purchase failures to compare whether and when each rule would notify the engineer. Check the failures that would no longer trigger an alert.

The quieter rule would have missed a problem that needed help.

On Thursday, customers could not complete purchases. The current rule notified the engineer. The proposed rule would have stayed silent because the failures never became common enough to trigger it.

Do not adopt this version of the rule. Keep the current rule while looking for a way to reduce needless interruptions without hiding purchase failures that need attention.

The reduction from 18 alerts to 4 is still a real result. It does not justify a change that would miss a customer problem the team needs to detect.

How this could sound in the report
“The proposed rule would reduce last week’s alerts from 18 to 4, but it would miss Thursday’s purchase failure. I recommend keeping the current rule while we revise the proposal. This version does not meet our need to notify someone when customers need help.”

A useful follow-up: Revise the rule, then check it against past failures and short-lived problems before adopting it.

The missing connection

Reducing interruptions and detecting customer problems are different requirements. The count answers one question; the failure check tells us whether this particular change meets another essential requirement.

The trial worked for recent orders. Must older orders hold up the whole move?

An online shop is preparing to move its order records to a new database—the system that stores them. In a practice run, recent orders move correctly: the amounts match, and staff can find and read the orders in the new system.

Older orders are stored differently and were not included in the trial. A colleague says, “We haven’t tested those orders, so we can’t move anything.” Before deciding, the team needs to know whether all orders must move together.

The proposal

“Older orders haven’t been tested. The whole move must wait.”

Does the shop need every order in the new system at once? Or can recent orders move while older orders stay available where they are?

What does the proposed move require?

The untested older orders limit the move. They do not have to stop it.

Recent orders passed the trial. The team also checked that staff can find the right orders across both systems, that the records stay correct, and that the move can be undone safely. Older orders have not been tested.

Move the tested recent orders in small groups and check that they remain correct. Keep older orders available in the old system until their own trial succeeds.

The evidence supports this smaller step. It does not support moving the untested older orders yet.

How this could sound in the report
“The trial supports moving the tested recent orders. We have also checked that using both systems keeps the orders accessible and correct, and that the move can be undone safely. I recommend a limited move while older orders stay in the old system. They need their own trial before moving.”

A useful follow-up: Check that orders stay accessible and correct during the limited move. Test examples of the different older formats before including them.

This plan needs evidence the trial did not provide.

The recent orders still passed the trial. But the shop cannot operate with older orders left behind, and those orders have not been tested.

Hold the complete move. Test examples of the different older formats, fix any problems, and check that the complete move can be undone safely before scheduling it.

The same evidence gap now affects records required for the next step. It blocks this plan, even though the result for recent orders is still valid.

How this could sound in the report
“Recent orders passed the trial, but this plan requires older orders in the new system too. They have not been tested, so I recommend holding the complete move until their trial succeeds and a safe way to undo the move has been checked.”

A useful follow-up: Test examples of the different older formats and fix any problems. Verify the complete move and a safe way to undo it before setting a date.

The missing connection

“Older orders are untested” describes a limit of the trial. Whether that means moving fewer orders or waiting altogether depends on what the next step requires.

02 / Take the idea with you

Name what the gap changes.

A missing check does not always mean “stop,” and a favorable number does not always mean “go.” Explain what the evidence supports: another check, a smaller step, or holding the proposed change.

Wording to take with you

We found [result] when we tested [cases]. This supports [interpretation], but leaves [gap]. Because the next step needs [requirement], I recommend [action and scope].
